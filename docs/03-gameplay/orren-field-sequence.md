# Orren · The long board

Working encounter for **A1, Hold the quay**. At Gauge Steps there are two places a shallow ferry can nose in. The worker can make a level boarding for a group of passengers or get the harvest quilts out before their store takes water. These are people the worker will see again on the return from Keld.

## Before the stair drops

There is an old game at Gauge Steps. While a boat stands at the stair, try to pitch a little rope ring over the toe of the figurehead. A heron used to stand on this boat's bow. Its neck broke off against the stone years ago. The crew kept the big wooden foot. The passenger waiting for the worker's elbow can land a ring on it from a sitting position. One optional throw with the ordinary aim and interact controls. On a miss she fishes it back with a boathook laid across her knees. On a hit she calls down to the deckhand, “That's mine, by the way.” He pitches it back.

Play this in the quiet before the fixed stamp handoff. The worker hands the runner the stamped stub with its scope still empty and offers the passenger an elbow while the board is level. The deckhand splits the brass tally with the shore hand as the crew makes ready for a run it owes back here. Let the shore hand hang her half in its socket in view of the worker. The runner with the dispatch stub has time to reach South Cut. When the extra stroke comes, hold the camera at the landing. The heron's foot rises past the stone step where the passenger was sitting. Yesterday's survey cut still shows on the bedrock post. You could have run your thumbnail along it and straight onto the stair cheek. Now your nail hits a bare patch of stair.

## The shore the player can walk

Coming downhill to work, pass a boy spreading his coat for his little brother to sleep on in the uphill shelter. At the **head of Gauge Steps**, stand with the water ahead. The dry stair behind climbs to the service desk. To the left, a shallow cart ramp doubles back along the face of Low Quay. Its upper turn stands level with a ferry deck on this tide. This is where people who need a level board can still embark. To the right a farm drain opens into the basin. Its stone towpath goes past a bend to the harvest bunkroom in **Reedward**. Pickers sleep there on raised shelves during harvest. A low hatch lets a boat take a season's bedding in one load. Today the folded quilts are wanted in uphill rooms where other households have already gone. A narrow ridge path out the back takes one carrier at a time. Two people are already carrying what they can.

The landing at the bottom of the steps looks straight up the ramp to its shelter. Walk a few paces right along the verge and the first quilt comes into view at the hatch. One broad pale slab crosses the shelf and serves both berths. Beyond it a section of reed fence has fallen across the mud, leaving a gap back to the open beds. There is space to dodge sideways along the slab and reach either berth by the dry verge behind it.

Two of Tessa's three hulls have their loads and are leaving by the outer face of the quay. The one on the inside carries the long boarding plank for the level approach and draws little enough water to reach the quilt hatch. At this height either berth works. Once laden, she must unload at the receiving landing before she can come back. By then water coming up the drain will have reached the quilts, and even the long board will tilt too steeply at the ramp. The group at the ramp can wait out the night on the terrace under the eaves. At the hatch the bundles are already tied for the families in the uphill shelter.

## The first rush

The water from a drowned burrow lifts the discarded husk of a Bridleshell. It comes out sideways, turns over, and goes on floating. A live scout comes out of the same hole and immediately gets its feet under it. Its first rush runs along the shelf, away from the crowd. It lowers both ridges on its foreplate and scrapes a wedge into the silt. Once it drives forward it holds that line. Step aside and hit the pale hinge while it turns. Watch its feet. The shell can sidle almost before the hinge folds.

As the scout comes round again, its feet skitter over the loose reeds. From the safe stair a missed dodge is legible in the long furrow that ends where the worker stood. A basic strike on its hinge sends the scout sideways towards the fence gap. It runs for the beds if the way is open. It can also fall on the shelf. Give a fleeing animal a moment to scramble through the fence and disappear into the reeds.

## Give the ferry a berth

A broad-backed Bridleshell Mason has climbed onto the shelf between ramp and drain. The pilot keeps her boat standing off while it occupies the landing. Before the next fight the worker can walk the dry verge, look at either destination and hear the two crews.

At the ramp a woman has made a seat for her wife by laying their cottage door across two boxes. “Can we take the door as well? I'll only have to come back for it.” Tessa measures it against the board with her eye. “On edge. Hinges in.” At the hatch the keeper beats a quilt against the boards to attract attention. Two of the bundles are tied with little red wool loops. Her brother holds up his left hand from the deck. She tells him, “Yours is the one with the knots. Mam's is the nice one.”

Examine either post for the pilot's signals. A deliberate **“Board the ramp passengers”** or **“Load Reedward's dry bedding”** calls her in. Until the call, the worker can walk freely between them. On the call, the crew starts working the boat round to that side on oars. The shore hand stays behind the post with the loose end of the mooring line. The crew need that stretch of bank clear before they can lay the board. Save the berth when the boat turns.

**Clear the ramp.** Work up the shallow incline, staying between the Mason and the boat. The first time its foreplate goes down, the wide scrape points up the ramp. Lead the charge onto the broad slab and sidestep towards the post. The Mason's broad plate catches between the straight cart ruts. Until it can lift one edge clear there is room to strike its rear joint. A light scout comes up out of the mud behind it. Line up its rush across the Mason's flank. The impact lifts the Mason's plate out of the ruts. It backs onto the mud broadside. Defeat it there, or stand between the Mason and the gap in the fence. As it commits, step off that line. It drives through to the reeds, then keeps going into cover. Either defeat or retreat clears an animal. The passengers wait under cover until both are clear.

**Clear the hatch.** Here the turn pinches down to a towpath. The Mason's plating faces the mouth of the drain. Swing round the inside of the bend by the dry upper verge and come down behind it. A scout scrapes at that higher end. There is room to let its rush pass downhill into the Mason, or to fight the two in succession using the bend to break their view. The path of a committed rush stays straight. From the upper verge its whole length should be visible. If two silhouettes cannot be seen at this camera scale, bring the scout out after the first charge. At the open end of the towpath the fence gap is downhill. Stand in front of it, let the shell commit and step up onto the verge.

| Shorer | Seat a foot in the slab, stand off to one side and receive the charge across the braced edge. Step with the turning shell to strike the hinge. The soft verge gives ground under a stake. |

Basic strikes, a dodge and a clear patch of verge suffice for both berths. A fall before the pilot is called restarts the first rush. A fall after restores the chosen approach before the plank goes down. Neither spends a tide.

At the ramp the shore hand can now bring the long board across at deck height. The woman takes her wife's elbow. They make it down the length of the board side by side. The worker catches the light end of their door while the crew turn it on edge. Across the drain the harvest crew are still pitching quilts uphill one by one. Water works its way up the stack.

At the hatch the keeper drops the knotted quilt straight over her brother's head. He makes a show of settling down to sleep in it. The next bundle catches him squarely in the chest. Lay the long board to the hatch lip and the crew can slide whole bundles down it. On the terrace the waiting passengers carry their boxes and the cottage door in under the eaves. Tessa goes up herself to tell them which boat will come after the night.

Let the worker depart for South Cut after the first load crosses. A return before sailing finds the crew stowing that load. After departure the boat and its cargo have gone and the berth stays cleared. A repaired skiff in *The Forty-Sixth Place* can make its extra passenger trip at the established freight cost. Its passengers climb over a gunwale and sit two to a bench. Its seats cannot take the harvest stack, and the drop over its side is too much for the level-boarding group.

## Second high · Coming back from Keld

Come in looking up at the dry terrace. The lower ramp is shut to carts. Let the player cross this space on the way to Ada and the Sere boat.

- **Ramp first.** Wet quilts hang doubled over the rail. The keeper and her brother take one between them to wring it. When they let go, the cloth slowly twists itself back up. Two children have slept in one coat in the uphill shelter. The older boy puts his right arm through one sleeve and gives the younger the left. They have to go sideways through the door like that.
- **Hatch first.** Dry quilts have already come up to the receiving homes. Under the eaves the door still spans its boxes. Four hinge screws lie in the dip worn under the knocker. The later boat has come. One of the women insists on seeing her wife aboard before going back for the door. Take its light end for her and carry it a few steps to the new boarding place. She tips the screws into her pocket before she gives the door up.

If the beached skiff was restored, its returning passenger is among the people handling bedding at the second high. Both branches leave time for Ada's exceptional ebb. Down on the ordinary ferry a deckhand is trying the ring game from the wrong end of the boat. He has to throw over his own head. His mates have pulled in the boathook to see how he does.
