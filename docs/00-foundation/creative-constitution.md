# Creative constitution — Alasia: Lost Isles

**ID:** FND-001 · **Status:** WORKING · **Owner:** Creative direction / narrative design · **Version:** 0.1

## 1. The seed — before an atlas, pantheon, or class list

**One sentence:** A coastal society has learned to lift one threatened island by transferring geological strain to another; it has survived for generations by concealing whose land settles instead.

**Player promise:** The player does not simply defeat an evil that arrived from outside. They discover an arrangement that keeps real people alive at the expense of other real people, and must use dangerous expertise to change its terms while fighting through the consequences of its failures.

**Controlling question:** *Who is allowed to decide which shore bears the weight?*

This is neither a post-apocalyptic ruin tour nor a morality fable where one righteous lever solves everything. The infrastructure really does prevent famine and mass displacement. Its secret allocation really is indefensible. Both must remain true.

**The smallest playable expression:** A tide gauge on Orren shows that the shore has dropped although the tide itself has not risen. A field-worker must cross a flooded district, fight creatures driven from their habitat by the pressure failure, rescue people, and discover that an allocation valve was deliberately opened. The evidence points beyond the island.

## 2. Seven binding creative principles

| ID | Principle | Observable consequence | Rejection test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C-01 | Every power has a source and recipient | Combat and infrastructure use the same strain law at different scales | The power creates unlimited energy, people, matter, or land |
| C-02 | Geography precedes content | Routes, food, labor, tides and trade explain settlements and dungeons | A location exists only to provide a snowy/swampy/desert level |
| C-03 | Institutions contain people, not alignments | Each major opponent has a defensible factual premise and a culpable choice | The institution is secretly run by a demon or simply enjoys cruelty |
| C-04 | The player acts; the story responds | Missions change evidence, supply, escape routes or known facts | A quest could be removed without affecting the next event |
| C-05 | Every starter class has its own combat grammar | Distinct resources, decisions, positioning and timing; shared world technology | Replace its implements with a sword, bow or wand and nothing changes |
| C-06 | Combat readable at speed | Immediate impact, fair tells, spatial clarity, real tactical counterplay | Darkness, spectacle or noise hides damage and hazards |
| C-07 | The world is made by labor | Infrastructure has maintenance crews, materials, receipts and costs | Giant ruins or magical apparatus appear without builders or upkeep |

## 3. Tone and imaginative boundaries

**Tone:** Dread born from maintenance failure; rain that exposes poor masonry; people who talk about food, boats, concrete obligations and places where their families lived. Rich historical texture with sparse, precise wonder. Characters may laugh, bargain or love without breaking the dark register.

**Not:** generic angels and demons, a named prophecy, a once-per-era chosen hero, dimension-hopping, indiscriminate skull décor, omnipotent gods, memory erasure as a convenient substitute for political concealment, or a surprise that makes earlier choices meaningless.

**Visual inheritance from the ARPG genre:** isometric/top-down framing, dramatic silhouette readability, responsive attacks, dense hostile groups, meaningful loot and deliberate high-contrast combat effects. **Distinctive execution:** labor architecture, sediment and tensile mechanics, exposed tidal flats, physical routing lines, quiet civic violence. Do not emulate a recognizable branded HUD silhouette, class iconography, signature creature design or map composition.

**Mature content:** violence and bodily harm should serve intelligible stakes, never replace character writing. Harm to civilians must have consequences and must not become a cheap reward mechanic.

## 4. Narrative hierarchy

The story must satisfy, in order: **physical causality → institutional incentives → personal choices → historical records → present-day stakes → quests and combat**. Theme is the consequence of this hierarchy, not a speech pasted onto it.

For any proposed island, profession, conflict, enemy or relic, submit six answers:

1. Which world law or specific historical event produced it?
2. Who maintains or benefits from it? What do they eat, trade and fear losing?
3. Why does it exist *here* rather than on any other island?
4. How has it changed in the past forty years?
5. What does the player do with it (verb, choice, risk, reward)?
6. What would break elsewhere if it were removed?

If answers 1, 2 and 3 are thin, it is not worldbuilding yet.

## 5. Design pillar contracts

**Pillar P1 — A world you can prove.** New information is anchored in measurable levels, work orders, physical repairs, tax receipts, oral accounts and the difference between published charts and actual shorelines. Rare mysteries are not excuses to discard the rules.

**Pillar P2 — Movement that has consequence.** The battlefield is a system of contacts, loading, timing and connections. Positioning matters without forcing players to stop for extended puzzles mid-combat.

**Pillar P3 — Builds that change verbs.** Itemization changes how force is captured, observed, carried, linked and released—not only damage percentage. A beginner can succeed without a build guide; mastery reveals new routes.

**Pillar P4 — People before kingdoms.** The crucial unit of conflict is a named boat, a census column, a warehouse full of grain, a mother who needs dry ground for burial, and a regulator whose action moves a coastline.

**Pillar P5 — Restraint makes spectacle.** Let empty stone, damp cloth and small metallic sounds establish scale so major pressure releases feel enormous.

## 6. First-release hypothesis (subject to G0)

- **Form:** single-player authored campaign, top-down 3D browser game for desktop first. Co-op, live service, trading economy and procedural endgame are explicit **not-in-first-slice** items.
- **Audience:** players who want fast, challenging ARPG combat and authored narrative rather than a passive lore codex.
- **Scope:** four standing islands and two lost sites are *world geography*, not a promise of four giant explorable maps. One Orren district is the first vertical slice.
- **Three starter professions:** Shorer, Sounder and Linewright. They share the setting's physical technology; they do not share interchangeable gameplay.
- **Story:** one coherent campaign spine with constrained consequential divergence and honest downstream cost. No fictionalized unlimited-branch promise.
- **Accessibility:** remappable controls, legible telegraphs independent of color and sound, subtitle support, scalable UI and assist options are first-order design concerns.

## 7. Quality definition, not marketing language

**“AAA-level” is the acceptance bar for craft, not a claim about our budget, staff or current implementation.** The work earns the label only if evidence exists: a convincing original seed; coherent geography and economy; tested readable combat at density; excellent animation and audio feel; authored, revised dialogue; reliable modern-browser performance; accessible UI; repeatable quality assurance; provenance of every external asset.

A beautiful mood board is insufficient. An exhaustive design document is insufficient. A vertical slice that *demonstrates* the promises is required. Production must cut quantity before sacrificing coherence, interaction fidelity or authorial care.

## 8. Approval and change control

**LOCKED:** project title only. **WORKING:** all proposed locations, history, laws in implementation detail, NPC names, class kits, architecture and numbers. **REJECTED:** ideas recorded in the critique register. **APPROVED:** only after owner review, explicit version update, named evidence and dependency audit.

Change the physical law only by simultaneously revising world, atlas, history, cast, campaign, classes, enemies, visuals, economy and technical requirements. Do not silently introduce contradictory exceptions.
